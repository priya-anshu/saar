CHAPTER NOTES

Arithmetic Expressions

When a calculation has more than one operation — like addition and multiplication both appearing in the same problem — the order in which we do the steps changes the final answer. This chapter fixes one universal order that everyone agrees to follow, so that any arithmetic expression (a combination of numbers joined by operations such as +, −, ×, ÷) always has exactly one correct value.

1

Introduction

Consider the calculation 3 + 4 × 2. Solved left to right, this gives (3+4)×2 = 14. Solved by multiplying first, it gives 3+(4×2) = 11. Since a calculation should have only one correct answer, mathematicians agreed on a fixed order of operations that everyone must follow — and the correct answer here is 11.

An arithmetic expression is a meaningful combination of numbers and the operations +, −, ×, ÷ (and sometimes brackets), without an equals sign. For example, 15 − 6 + 2 × 3 is an arithmetic expression, while x = 5 is not (it is called an equation).

2

Building Blocks of an Expression

Operands

The numbers on which an operation is performed. In 8 + 5, the operands are 8 and 5.

Operators

The symbols that show which operation to perform: +, −, ×, ÷.

Terms

Parts of an expression separated by + or − signs. In 6 × 3 + 4, the terms are "6 × 3" and "4".

Value

The single number obtained after fully evaluating (working out) an expression.

3

Order of Operations (BODMAS)

The agreed order of operations is remembered using the word BODMAS: Brackets, Orders (powers and roots), Division, Multiplication, Addition, Subtraction.

&

→

Work in this order: Brackets → Orders (powers) → Division/Multiplication (whichever appears first, left to right) → Addition/Subtraction (whichever appears first, left to right).

Important detail

Division and Multiplication have equal priority — do whichever appears first, reading left to right. The same is true for Addition and Subtraction.

Worked example

Evaluate: 20 ÷ 4 × 2 + 3² − 1

Step 1 (Orders): 3² = 9 → 20 ÷ 4 × 2 + 9 − 1
Step 2 (Division/Multiplication, left to right): 20 ÷ 4 = 5, then 5 × 2 = 10 → 10 + 9 − 1
Step 3 (Addition/Subtraction, left to right): 10 + 9 = 19, then 19 − 1 = 18
Answer: 18

4

Working with Brackets

Brackets are used to group part of an expression so it is worked out first, before anything else — even before division or multiplication elsewhere in the same expression.

BracketNameUsed when
( )Round brackets / parenthesesInnermost grouping
[ ]Square bracketsAround round brackets
{ }Curly bracketsOutermost grouping

When brackets are nested (placed one inside another), always solve the innermost bracket first, then work outward.

Worked example

Evaluate: 2 + {10 − [6 ÷ (3 − 1)]}

Step 1 — innermost ( ): 3 − 1 = 2 → 2 + {10 − [6 ÷ 2]}
Step 2 — [ ]: 6 ÷ 2 = 3 → 2 + {10 − 3}
Step 3 — { }: 10 − 3 = 7 → 2 + 7
Answer: 9

Brackets can change the value completely

(5 + 3) × 2 = 16, but 5 + (3 × 2) = 11 — same numbers and operations, different bracket placement, different answer.

5

Simplifying Expressions Step by Step

The safest way to simplify a long expression is to rewrite it after every single step, rather than trying to do several operations at once in your head.

Worked example

Simplify: 45 − 3 × (8 + 2) ÷ 6

45 − 3 × (8 + 2) ÷ 6
= 45 − 3 × 10 ÷ 6  (bracket first)
= 45 − 30 ÷ 6  (multiplication, left to right)
= 45 − 5  (division)
= 40

6

Comparing Expressions

Sometimes we can tell which of two expressions is larger without fully calculating both — by reasoning about what each operation does.

Useful reasoning tricks

  • Adding the same number to both sides of a comparison does not change which one is bigger.
  • Multiplying (or dividing) both sides by the same positive number does not change which one is bigger.
  • If two expressions share a common part, compare only the parts that differ.

Worked example

Without fully calculating, compare: 25 + 48 × 3 and 25 + 47 × 3.
Both expressions share "25 +" and "× 3" — only 48 and 47 differ, and 48 > 47. So 25 + 48 × 3 > 25 + 47 × 3.

7

Turning Statements into Expressions

Word problems can be translated into arithmetic expressions by identifying the operations described and the order in which they must happen.

Worked examples

  • "Add 7 and 5, then multiply the result by 3" → (7 + 5) × 3 = 36
  • "Multiply 7 and 5, then add 3 to the result" → (7 × 5) + 3 = 38
  • "Subtract the product of 4 and 3 from 20" → 20 − (4 × 3) = 8

Whenever a sentence says "the result of..." or "then...", it is usually a signal to use brackets around the part that must be calculated first.

8

Real Life Uses

Shopping bills

Total cost = (3 × 40) + (2 × 25) — price × quantity for each item, then added.

Recipes

Doubling a recipe: (2 + 3 + 1) × 2 gives the new total of combined ingredients.

Spreadsheets

Formulas like =(A1+A2)*A3 follow the exact same order-of-operations rules.

Calculators

Scientific calculators automatically apply BODMAS; basic ones may calculate strictly left to right, giving different results.

9

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Solving strictly left to right and ignoring BODMAS, e.g. treating 6 + 4 × 2 as (6+4)×2 = 20 instead of the correct 6 + 8 = 14.
  • Assuming multiplication always comes before division, or addition always before subtraction — they have equal priority and are done left to right.
  • Working outward instead of starting from the innermost bracket in nested expressions.
  • Forgetting to simplify a bracket completely before using its value in the rest of the expression.
  • Losing track of a negative sign when removing brackets, e.g. 10 − (4 − 2) is 10 − 2 = 8, not 10 − 4 − 2 = 4.

Practice Questions

  1. Evaluate: 18 − 4 × 3 + 6 ÷ 2
  2. Evaluate: (15 + 5) ÷ 4 × 2
  3. Evaluate: 3 × {12 − [4 + (2 × 3)]}
  4. Insert brackets in 6 + 2 × 5 to make the value 40.
  5. Without fully calculating, state which is greater: 18 × 6 − 9 or 18 × 6 − 7, and why.
  6. Translate into an expression: "Divide the sum of 24 and 12 by 6."
  7. Evaluate: 5² − 3 × (7 − 4) + 8 ÷ 4

Quick Revision Sheet

  • BODMAS: Brackets → Orders (powers) → Division/Multiplication (left to right) → Addition/Subtraction (left to right).
  • Division and Multiplication share priority; so do Addition and Subtraction — always work left to right between them.
  • Nested brackets: solve the innermost one first, then move outward — ( ) → [ ] → { }.
  • Rewrite the expression after every step to avoid losing track of signs or values.
  • Two expressions can sometimes be compared by reasoning about their shared parts, without full calculation.
  • Phrases like "the result of" or "then" in word problems usually signal where brackets are needed.
